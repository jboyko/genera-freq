# The frequency with which large genera are expected to evolve

- "Given enough data on sizes of genera having different geographical distributions, patterns of morphology and of cytogenetic diversity, one might be able to calculate the frequency with which large genera could be expected to evolve. I do not believe that this is possible with the information we have today." (Stebbins 1981)

## Abstract

Stebbins suggested that to calculate how often large genera could be expected to evolve, one would need sufficient data on the sizes of genera along with their geographical distributions, patterns of morphology, and patterns of cytogenetic diversity

# Introduction

Some plant genera contain thousands of species. *Astragalus*, *Senecio*, *Euphorbia*, *Solanum*, and a handful of others account for a disproportionate share of vascular plant diversity. Why these lineages and not others is one of the oldest questions in comparative biology, and one that remains largely unanswered. Stebbins (1981) argued that no single factor — geographic range, chromosomal lability, floral architecture, dispersal strategy — could explain all large genera. Instead, different genera appeared to owe their richness to different "opportunistic interactions" between gene complexes and environmental conditions. He suggested that if sufficient data on genus sizes, geographic distributions, morphological patterns, and cytogenetic diversity were available, one might be able to calculate the frequency with which large genera could be expected to evolve. He did not believe this was possible with the information available at the time.

Four decades later, the data landscape has changed substantially. Global taxonomic databases now provide accepted species counts for tens of thousands of genera. Digitized herbarium records, molecular phylogenies, chromosome databases, and trait compilations offer coverage of geographic range, clade age, morphological diversity, and cytogenetic variation at scales Stebbins could not have anticipated. The analytical tools have advanced as well. What Stebbins framed as a qualitative puzzle — which combinations of traits tend to produce large genera — can now be formulated as a quantitative prediction problem: given a genus with trait profile X, what is the probability that it reaches an exceptional size?

Before this question can be addressed, however, a more basic problem must be resolved. What counts as a large genus? The literature offers no consistent answer. Studies variously treat genera above 500 species, above 1,000 species, or in the top percentile of their clade as "large" or "mega-diverse," but these thresholds are arbitrary. They are chosen by convention or convenience, and they carry consequences. Any analysis that asks what predicts membership in a size class inherits the assumptions embedded in how that class was defined. Different thresholds yield different membership lists, different correlates, and potentially different conclusions. The question Stebbins posed cannot be answered rigorously if the dependent variable — what it means to be a large genus — is defined by fiat.

We address this problem by grounding genus-size categories in the statistical structure of the distribution itself. Species-richness across vascular plant genera is heavily right-skewed: most genera contain fewer than ten species, while a long tail extends into the hundreds and thousands. We use the framework of Clauset et al. (2009) to estimate the threshold above which this extreme tail begins, and then apply the Head/Tail Breaks algorithm (Jiang & Yin 2010) to partition the tail into nested size classes. The resulting categories — small, medium, large, and mega-diverse — are not imposed on the data. They emerge from it. Their boundaries are reproducible, grounded in the hierarchical structure of the distribution, and independent of subjective judgment about what "large" ought to mean.

With a principled classification in hand, we turn to Stebbins' question. We compile data on [geographic range / clade age / chromosomal diversity / growth habit diversity / floral structural diversity / dispersal syndrome / — adjust to match final trait set] for each genus and ask which traits, and which combinations of traits, predict membership in the upper size classes. Following Stebbins' insight that no single factor explains all large genera, we examine whether distinct trait profiles characterize different mega-diverse lineages — whether, in effect, there are multiple routes to exceptional richness. The goal is not to identify a single cause of genus size but to estimate, as Stebbins envisioned, the frequency with which genera possessing particular trait combinations can be expected to reach extreme species-richness.

## Methods
### Defining thresholds for large genera

Genus species-richness in vascular plants is strongly right-skewed. Most genera contain few species. A small number contain hundreds or thousands. Rather than adopt an arbitrary size threshold, we sought a principled basis for distinguishing unusually large genera from the background distribution.

We analyzed accepted species counts for 13,803 genera with at least one accepted species from the World Flora Online database. Because the distribution is heavily right-skewed, standard summary statistics such as the mean and standard deviation do not adequately characterize its structure. We therefore used the framework of Clauset et al. (2009) to estimate $x_{\min}$, the threshold above which the extreme right tail begins. This method fits a power-law model to the upper tail by maximum-likelihood estimation and selects the $x_{\min}$ that minimizes the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the fitted model and the empirical distribution. The resulting threshold provides a data-driven estimate of where genus sizes transition from the body of the distribution into the heavy tail. We note that the tail is statistically consistent with both power-law and log-normal models (log-likelihood ratio test, $p = $ ...). The classification does not depend on which model generated the tail — only on the tail being heavy and hierarchically structured, which it demonstrably is.

To identify finer structure within this tail, we applied Head/Tail Breaks (Jiang & Yin 2010) to the genera above $x_{\min}$. The algorithm partitions recursively at the arithmetic mean: at each step, genera above the mean are retained and the process repeats as long as the above-mean subset constitutes less than 40% of the current partition, ensuring that each successive level remains heavy-tailed. The result is a set of nested size classes — small, medium, large, and mega-diverse — whose boundaries emerge from the statistical structure of the data rather than from subjective judgment.

## Results
### Genus size thresholds and tiers
The estimated x_min was 168 species. This defined a tail of 402 genera, or 2.9% of all genera. Above this threshold, the power-law and log-normal models were statistically indistinguishable. This is a common result with finite empirical data (Clauset et al. 2009). We treat 168 species as the entry point to the tail. Genera above this threshold are rarer than expected under the log-normal body of the distribution.

When Head/Tail Breaks (Jiang & Yin 2010) was applied to genera above x_min, the procedure yielded two thresholds: 420 species, separating the top 0.8% of all genera from the rest of the tail, and 883 species, separating the top 0.2%. A third iteration did not satisfy the heavy-tail criterion and was not carried forward.

We define four tiers of genus size based on these thresholds. Minor genera have fewer than 168 species and make up the log-normal body of the distribution (n = 13,401). Moderate genera have 168–419 species (n = 292). Big genera have 420–882 species (n = 78). Megadiverse genera have 883 or more species (n = 32). The two upper thresholds align with conventional botanical definitions of big and megadiverse genera. They provide a statistical foundation for size categories that have previously been used only descriptively.

## Discussion
## Interpreting genus size thresholds

The $x_{\min}$ threshold estimated by the Clauset et al. (2009) framework identifies where the extreme right tail of the genus-size distribution begins. Our likelihood ratio test showed that, above this point, the power-law and log-normal models are statistically indistinguishable. The data are consistent with both. It is possible that a single stochastic process generates the entire distribution, including the extreme values. It is also possible that the generating process differs above $x_{\min}$. The analysis cannot distinguish these scenarios, and the classification we derive from it does not require resolving them. What matters is that the distribution has a heavy, hierarchically structured right tail, and that $x_{\min}$ provides a principled, reproducible estimate of where that tail begins.

Genera above $x_{\min}$ (168 species) are rare and far from the mode regardless of which model generated them. The biological interest of these genera does not rest on whether their sizes are statistically anomalous under one model or another. It rests on the observation that at extreme sizes, the biological assumptions underlying any simple stochastic model — rate homogeneity, independence from genus size, ecological equivalence among lineages — are most likely to break down. A genus of 20 species and a genus of 80 species may differ substantially in their biology, but both fall within the range that stochastic compounding of speciation, extinction, clade age, geographic range, and ecological opportunity can readily produce. A genus of 1,000 species has persisted and diversified across timescales and geographic contexts where the constancy of those rates becomes increasingly difficult to maintain. Whether such genera were produced by the same background process operating under unusually favorable circumstances, or whether distinct mechanisms — sustained high diversification, access to broad adaptive zones, repeated opportunities for geographic isolation, taxonomic oversplitting, or some combination — drove their exceptional richness, cannot be resolved by distributional analysis alone. Determining which account applies, and whether it varies across lineages, is a question for phylogenetic and biogeographic investigation.

The Head/Tail Breaks classification that follows from $x_{\min}$ partitions the tail into nested size classes whose boundaries reflect the hierarchical structure of the data. These classes (small, medium, large, mega-diverse) are not claims about distinct biological processes. They are a principled descriptive framework for organizing genera along the richness spectrum — one that can be applied consistently across clades and datasets and that replaces arbitrary thresholds with boundaries grounded in the statistical structure of the distribution itself.
